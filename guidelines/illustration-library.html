The mark

Settled: the wordmark stays as it is. It remains the mark on all 38 pages of the brand book and on every applied page. The five painted files that arrived named as logos contain no readable brand name — two are ringed seals reading A over B, one is a painted panel with a concentric ring, one is a four-up of ornaments. They are now filed as emblems, not logotypes: available as a maker's stamp on packaging, a tag, or an avatar, always with the wordmark carrying the name.

The pairing rule

An emblem may appear without the wordmark only where the name is already given by context — a profile picture beside the handle, a stamp on a box that carries a printed tag. Everywhere else the wordmark leads and the emblem is subordinate, at no more than a third of its width.

Nine positions — retired

Superseded. Kept as a record, not a proposal. All nine were answers to the wrong question — how to make a photograph look like a painting. They should not look alike: they should do different jobs and never occupy the same slot. The rule that replaces all of this, plus the shotlist and a surface-by-surface table, is in Image jobs. Position A
Shared ground

Bring the photography to the paintings. Every product shot is taken on the same flat pigment the paintings use — one seamless teal or orange field, no props, no linen, no wood. A photograph and a painting side by side then read as two pictures of one world.

What it costs

A full reshoot, and a paint chip sent to the photographer. Nothing already on the account survives.

Position B
Separate rooms

They never share a view. Painting owns the collection headers, the place, the story, the packaging. Photography is pushed into a deliberately clerical specification page — stone ground, form-green ink, hairlines — so the contrast is a decision rather than an accident.

What it costs

The shop pages lose all warmth. A visitor sees two brands unless the typography is doing heavy work.

Position C
Framed inside

Photographs are always inset inside a painting, never beside one, so a painting frames every photograph and the two are never equals. The painting is the picture; the photograph is what it contains.

What it costs

Nothing. Any existing photograph works the moment a painting surrounds it. Costs screen space.

Position D
Cut out onto paper

The photograph loses its background entirely. Each piece is silhouetted and dropped straight onto the lime page, so there is no photographic ground anywhere on the site — the paper is the only ground, exactly as it is for a painting.

What it costs

Cut-out work on every product shot, and it only works on pieces with a clean outline. Chains fail.

Position E
Two inks only

Photographs are printed in the brand's two inks and nothing else — teal in the shadows, orange in the light. They stop being photographs and become part of the palette. Detail survives; realism does not.

What it costs

Skin goes teal, which some people will hate. Silver reads as colour, not metal.

Position F
Pasted print

The photograph is a physical print laid on top of the painting — torn edge, slight rotation, a shadow where it lifts. The seam is deliberate. This is the scrapbook logic the paintings already have, extended to photography.

What it costs

Fussy. Reads as craft on one page and as a template on the twentieth. Needs restraint.

Position G
Always a pair

A photograph never appears alone. Every product shot is published with the painting of its place, same size, same crop, one hairline between them, captioned. The rule is arithmetic: one piece, two pictures, always.

What it costs

Doubles the image count on every page and needs a painting per piece. Not viable for a large catalogue.

Position H
Scale inversion

The painting is always big and the photograph is always small — a 64px chip, never more. The painting is the picture; the photograph is evidence that the thing exists. They cannot compete because they are never the same size.

What it costs

Customers want to see the product large. This position asks them to trust the painting instead.

Position I
The register

Nothing is restyled at all. Painting and photograph sit in one grid, separated by a single hairline, each with a Commissioner caption and a reference number underneath. The clerical apparatus is what unites them — the brand's own receipt logic doing the joining instead of colour.

What it costs

The quietest and the cheapest. Also the least distinctive: it works, but nobody will call it beautiful.

How to read these

A, D and E change what the camera does, so they need a reshoot or retouching before anything can be built. B, C, F, G, H and I change only what the page does, so any of them could be applied to the photographs already on the account this week. If you want the strongest brand and can afford a reshoot, A. If you want the strongest brand with no reshoot, C or H. If you want the system to look like the invoices it is built on, I.

The photographs in every demo are the free-licence stand-ins, not AORI's own. A and E are browser approximations of a print process — a real duotone and a real pigment-ground shot will both look better than what is rendered here.
